# Implications

The implications are limited to webinar design, evaluation practice, and the interpretation of participant feedback. They are recommendations grounded in the observed ratings and coded comments, not claims about effects beyond this respondent set.

## Implications for future webinar design

The strongest quantitative result concerned the clarity and organization of the presentation materials (mean = 4.7308; 98.08% agreement; 75.00% top-box). Future sessions should therefore retain a clear slide structure, explicit objectives, and organized examples. The well-organized item also performed strongly (mean = 4.7115), which provides additional support for preserving the session's visible structure.

The qualitative results point to a need to add practice without discarding the existing presentation format. More examples received 7 assignments across 7 respondents, more interaction received 6 assignments across 5 respondents, and more depth received 4 assignments across 4 respondents. A future session could respond by adding a short live debugging exercise, a deliberately flawed AI-generated code sample, or a guided test-writing activity. These suggestions address the reported requests for practice and participation; they should be evaluated in a later session rather than treated as proven interventions.

The learning-value codes also provide a content-planning signal. AI use was coded in 36 assignments across 25 respondents, software development in 22 assignments across 21 respondents, and TDD or software testing in 21 assignments across 17 respondents. Future materials can preserve these topics while making the human review process explicit: participants should see how a generated suggestion is checked against a requirement, tested, revised, and accepted or rejected. This implication follows from the content of the coded feedback and the study framework; it is not an inference that the participants mastered those practices.

Technical-quality comments were less frequent than the learning and delivery codes, but they were still present: 2 assignments involved 2 respondents. Microphone, connection, and screen-sharing checks should remain part of the delivery checklist because a small number of technical problems can interrupt an online session even when overall ratings are high.

## Implications for evaluation design

The current instrument captured immediate perceptions efficiently. The 13-item matrix measured reported experience, while the two summary items and four open-ended prompts supplied separate overall and explanatory evidence. For future use, the form should keep the direction of the matrix visually explicit. The two scale-direction cases show why a short instruction, consistent left-to-right ordering, and a visible example may be useful before the grid begins.

If the evaluation objective changes from documenting experience to estimating learning, the instrument should add an objective task, a pre/post measure, or a delayed follow-up. The present post-event ratings cannot measure competence, retention, transfer, or causal impact. A revised design should also specify the attendance denominator and response rate so that the proportion of attendees represented by the evaluation can be reported.

## Implications for mixed-methods reporting

The two evidence streams should continue to be reported together. High ratings summarize the immediate response, while coded comments explain what participants noticed and what they wanted next. Future reports should retain the distinction between coded assignments and respondents because multiple codes were permitted per answer. The current evidence matrix and decision prompts provide a reproducible format for that handoff: [`mixed_methods_evidence_matrix.csv`](../ANALYSIS/outputs/tables/mixed_methods_evidence_matrix.csv) and [`crispdm_decision_prompts.csv`](../ANALYSIS/outputs/tables/crispdm_decision_prompts.csv).

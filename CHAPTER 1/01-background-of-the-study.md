# Background of the Study

This section places the webinar examined in this report within current discussions of software testing, test-driven development (TDD), AI-assisted programming, and beginner-oriented computing education. It moves from software quality and testing practice to the instructional problem addressed by the activity. Claims about the webinar itself are limited to what is visible in the supplied evaluation artifact; details about the organizers, platform, presenters, schedule, and demonstration materials remain marked for verification.

## Software quality and testing in contemporary development

Software testing is part of a wider engineering process that includes planning, design, implementation, evaluation, defect communication, and quality improvement. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-1:2022 describes general concepts for software testing and connects testing with test plans, test design, execution, reporting, and defect management (International Organization for Standardization, International Electrotechnical Commission, & Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, 2022). The current SWEBOK Guide also treats testing as one knowledge area within software engineering rather than as a final inspection performed after coding (Washizaki & Olszewska, 2024). This view matters for beginners because it places testing close to requirements and design decisions, where assumptions can still be examined.

Quality is also broader than whether a program produces the expected output in a single example. ISO/IEC 25010:2023 presents a product-quality model that can be used to specify, measure, and evaluate software and other information and communication technology products (International Organization for Standardization & International Electrotechnical Commission, 2023b). The current ISTQB Foundation Level syllabus similarly presents testing as a context-dependent activity shaped by the product, lifecycle, risks, and business conditions (International Software Testing Qualifications Board, 2023). Together, these sources support a teaching approach in which learners ask what a system should do, how that expectation can be checked, and what evidence is needed before a change is accepted.

Testing is connected to security as well as functional correctness. The Secure Software Development Framework recommends that software producers integrate security practices into their development lifecycles and define roles and training for people who contribute to secure development (Souppaya et al., 2022). Risk management is especially relevant when AI tools are included in the workflow. ISO/IEC 23894:2023 gives guidance for managing risks in the development, production, deployment, and use of AI-related systems (International Organization for Standardization & International Electrotechnical Commission, 2023a). NIST's AI Risk Management Framework likewise presents trustworthiness as a concern across the AI lifecycle, including design, development, use, and evaluation (Tabassi, 2023). For AI-assisted coding, these principles imply that generated code should be inspected, tested, and reviewed rather than accepted because it appears plausible.

The security concern is not only theoretical. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications identifies prompt injection, insecure output handling, supply-chain weaknesses, sensitive-information disclosure, excessive agency, and overreliance among the risks that developers should consider when building or using large-language-model applications (OWASP Foundation, 2024). NIST's Generative AI Profile adds risk-management actions for generative AI systems and treats evaluation, governance, and human oversight as part of responsible use (Autio et al., 2024). These sources give a practical reason to connect introductory AI use with disciplined testing: beginners need to see that an AI assistant can speed up code production while still producing outputs that require verification.

## Test-driven development as a learning object

TDD is commonly taught as a short cycle in which the developer writes a failing test, writes enough production code to pass it, and then refactors while preserving the behavior already checked by the tests. The cycle is often described as red, green, and refactor. In an educational setting, the value of the cycle is not limited to the resulting test suite. It gives learners a repeated routine for translating a requirement into an executable expectation and then using feedback to revise the implementation.

Recent studies show why TDD should be presented with care. Sheikh (2022) reports an instructional design and efficacy study that combined automated unit testing and TDD in C++ programming education. Baldassarre et al. (2022) found that affective reactions to TDD were not uniform across experiments and that prior experience with unit testing helped explain differences in how participants responded to the practice. Staegemann et al. (2022) likewise organize the challenges of applying TDD around people, software, and process. These findings caution against presenting TDD as a guaranteed improvement in quality or productivity. A short beginner webinar should instead make the cycle visible, explain why each step is used, and give participants enough context to judge when it helps.

The current literature also examines the experience of using TDD, not only its technical outputs. Calais and Franzini (2023) discuss TDD in relation to developer experience and the repeated feedback created by short red-green-refactor cycles. Agha et al. (2023) review the relationship between TDD, program design, and software quality. These studies support an instructional emphasis on small examples, immediate feedback, and explanation of the reasoning behind each test. They do not, however, establish that a single webinar produces lasting TDD skill. A post-event questionnaire can document participant perceptions and self-reported value, but it cannot by itself demonstrate durable competence or causal learning effects.

## AI-assisted programming and the need for verification

AI-assisted programming includes tools that generate, complete, explain, translate, repair, or test code from natural-language prompts and existing program context. Recent systems have made code generation visible to a broad audience. Li et al. (2022) describe AlphaCode, a system evaluated on programming competitions, while OpenAI (2023) describes GPT-4 as a general-purpose multimodal model that can produce text and code. These examples show the technical reach of current models, but benchmark performance is not the same as reliable software engineering in a local repository.

Studies of developer interaction make that distinction clearer. Peng et al. (2023) report a controlled study of GitHub Copilot in which access to the tool was associated with faster completion on a focused programming task. Barke et al. (2023) describe two common interaction patterns: programmers may use an assistant to move faster when they already know what to do, or to explore options when they are unsure. Vaithilingam et al. (2022) found that users often valued AI-generated code as a starting point but also had difficulty understanding, editing, and debugging some suggestions. Pearce et al. (2022) found security weaknesses in a large sample of Copilot-generated programs, which reinforces the need for review and testing. The practical lesson for beginners is straightforward: an AI assistant can support a workflow, but the learner still needs a way to state requirements, inspect output, run tests, and explain the result.

Repository context and task complexity also affect AI-assisted development. Zhang et al. (2023) show that repository-level code completion benefits from retrieving relevant information from across a codebase rather than relying only on the current file. Hou et al. (2024) map a large body of research on large language models for software engineering and identify continuing questions about data, evaluation, and task-specific reliability. Jimenez et al. (2024) evaluate language models on SWE-bench, a benchmark built from real GitHub issues and pull requests, which requires models to modify a codebase and satisfy tests. Yang et al. (2024) similarly show that the interface through which an agent navigates files, edits code, and executes tests changes its software-engineering performance. These studies reinforce the need to teach AI-assisted coding as a human-guided process rather than as one-step code generation.

AI can also be applied directly to testing. Schäfer et al. (2024) evaluate TestPilot, an approach that uses large language models to generate JavaScript unit tests from source code and documentation. The study demonstrates that automated test generation is feasible, but it also makes clear that coverage and generated output require evaluation. A test that runs is not automatically a good test, and a passing test does not prove that the requirement was understood correctly. This is why TDD is a useful frame for beginner instruction: tests can function as explicit, inspectable statements of intended behavior while also giving learners a way to check AI-generated implementation suggestions.

## Teaching the topic to beginners

The intended audience for the present activity is described in the paper title as beginners in AI-assisted TDD. Beginner instruction needs a clear sequence, manageable examples, and language that does not assume professional experience. Computer Science Curricula 2023 places software development, artificial intelligence, professional practice, and responsible computing within a contemporary computing curriculum (Kumar et al., 2023). The current SWEBOK Guide and ISTQB syllabus provide complementary professional vocabulary for software engineering and testing, but a short webinar cannot reproduce a full course or certification program (International Software Testing Qualifications Board, 2023; Washizaki & Olszewska, 2024).

The educational context also has to account for the risks and opportunities of generative AI. Becker et al. (2023) describe both the opportunities and the challenges that code generation creates for introductory programming education. Denny et al. (2024) argue that computing educators need to reconsider teaching, assessment, and academic-integrity practices as generative AI becomes more capable. In a controlled study of novice learners, Kazemitabaar et al. (2023) found that access to an AI code generator supported code-authoring performance without reducing performance on later code-modification tasks, while also noting the importance of how the tool is used. These results are relevant to the webinar's beginner focus, but they should not be treated as evidence that every learner or every tool will produce the same outcome.

Online delivery adds another design issue. The OECD describes digital education as an ecosystem that includes tools, institutional arrangements, teaching and learning practices, assessment, and the people who make those systems meaningful (OECD, 2023). UNESCO's guidance on generative AI in education and research calls for human-centred use, attention to privacy, and institutional capacity to evaluate whether tools are educationally and ethically appropriate (Miao & Holmes, 2023). A webinar on AI-assisted testing therefore needs more than a tool demonstration. It needs an explanation of what the tool is doing, where it can fail, how learners should validate its output, and what evidence will be used to evaluate the session.

## Background synthesis

Current standards and research point in the same practical direction: software quality depends on explicit requirements, testing, review, and risk-aware development; AI tools can assist coding and testing but can also produce insecure, incomplete, or misunderstood outputs; and beginner instruction benefits from concrete routines with visible feedback. TDD provides one such routine, while participant evaluation provides limited evidence about how a webinar was received. The present report responds to that intersection without treating positive participant feedback as proof of learning, productivity, or long-term skill.

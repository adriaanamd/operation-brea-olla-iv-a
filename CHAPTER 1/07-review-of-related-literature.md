# Review of related literature

This review situates the webinar in four connected bodies of work: software quality and testing, test-driven development (TDD), AI-assisted software development, and computing education. The literature does not treat these areas as interchangeable. Testing literature focuses on how evidence about software behavior is produced; TDD literature examines a particular feedback routine; AI-assisted development literature evaluates tools and developer interaction; and computing-education literature considers what learners need when those tools enter instruction. Reading the areas together clarifies why a beginner-oriented session on AI-assisted TDD needs to teach both productivity and verification.

## Software quality and testing as evidence-producing practice

Contemporary testing guidance places testing within the software life cycle rather than at the end of coding. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-1:2022 describes testing through activities such as planning, test design, execution, reporting, and defect management. The *SWEBOK Guide* likewise treats testing as a software-engineering knowledge area, while the ISO/IEC 25010:2023 quality model frames quality as a set of characteristics that must be specified, measured, and evaluated. The ISTQB Foundation Level syllabus adds a contextual point: testing choices depend on the product, life cycle, risks, and intended use (International Organization for Standardization, International Electrotechnical Commission, & Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, 2022; Washizaki & Olszewska, 2024; International Organization for Standardization & International Electrotechnical Commission, 2023b; International Software Testing Qualifications Board, 2023).

This body of work matters to the present topic because it rejects the idea that a plausible output is sufficient evidence of quality. A test is useful when it is connected to an expectation and when its result informs a decision. The Secure Software Development Framework extends that logic to security by recommending that secure-development practices be integrated into the life cycle and supported through roles and training (Souppaya et al., 2022). For learners working with AI-generated code, the implication is practical: they need a repeatable way to state what the code should do, inspect what was produced, run checks, and respond to failures.

## Test-driven development and iterative feedback

TDD gives that verification problem a compact instructional form. The red–green–refactor cycle starts with a failing test, adds the smallest implementation that satisfies it, and then improves the code while preserving the checked behavior. The cycle connects requirements, implementation, and feedback in a sequence that learners can observe. It also gives an instructor a concrete way to demonstrate why tests are written before or alongside production code rather than treated as a final inspection.

Empirical and review studies, however, do not support presenting TDD as a universal solution. Sheikh (2022) reports a design and efficacy study of automated unit testing and TDD in C++ instruction, showing how the practice can be built into programming education. Agha et al. (2023) synthesize links between TDD, program design, and software quality. At the same time, Baldassarre et al. (2022) report variation in affective responses across experiments and identify prior testing experience as relevant to how participants respond. Staegemann et al. (2022) organize TDD challenges around people, software, and process, while Calais and Franzini (2023) connect TDD with developer experience and the repeated feedback of short cycles.

Taken together, these sources support a qualified instructional position. TDD is a useful object for showing how a developer can turn a requirement into an executable expectation and use feedback to revise code. The literature does not justify promising that one exposure will produce durable skill, improved productivity, or higher-quality software for every learner. A webinar therefore needs examples that make the reasoning behind each test visible, not only a demonstration of the final passing result.

## AI-assisted programming and human oversight

AI-assisted programming changes how code may be produced, but it does not remove the need for software-engineering judgment. Li et al. (2022) describe AlphaCode as a competition-level code-generation system, and OpenAI (2023) document GPT-4 as a general-purpose model capable of producing code among other forms of output. These systems show what code-generating models can do under particular tasks and evaluation conditions. They do not by themselves establish that generated code is correct, secure, maintainable, or appropriate for a learner’s local project.

Research on developer interaction explains why the distinction matters. Barke et al. (2023) describe programmers using code-generating models both to accelerate work when they already know the intended direction and to explore possibilities when they are uncertain. Vaithilingam et al. (2022) report that users valued generated code as a starting point but could struggle to understand, edit, and debug suggestions. Peng et al. (2023) associate Copilot access with faster completion in a focused task, while Pearce et al. (2022) identify security weaknesses in a large sample of generated code. These findings point in the same direction: speed and usefulness are conditional on the user’s ability to inspect and validate the result.

The software-engineering setting adds further constraints. Zhang et al. (2023) show the importance of repository-level information for code completion, rather than relying only on the current file. Hou et al. (2024) review the broader literature on large language models for software engineering and identify continuing questions about evaluation, data, and task-specific reliability. Jimenez et al. (2024) evaluate models on SWE-bench, where a system must modify a real repository in response to an issue and satisfy tests. Yang et al. (2024) examine agent–computer interfaces for automated software engineering. Schäfer et al. (2024) focus on automated unit-test generation and show why generated tests also require evaluation. The combined message is that AI assistance operates inside a technical and evaluative context; it is not a substitute for requirements analysis, code review, testing, or accountability.

Risk-management guidance reinforces this human-guided view. ISO/IEC 23894:2023 and NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework describe risk management across the AI life cycle, while the Generative AI Profile adds controls for evaluation, oversight, and responsible deployment (International Organization for Standardization & International Electrotechnical Commission, 2023a; Tabassi, 2023; Autio et al., 2024). OWASP Foundation (2024) identifies risks such as prompt injection, insecure output handling, sensitive-information disclosure, excessive agency, and overreliance in large-language-model applications. These sources do not prescribe a particular webinar format, but they explain why an introductory session should pair tool use with verification and responsible judgment.

## Computing education and novice learners

The educational literature adds the learner’s perspective. *Computer Science Curricula 2023* places software development, artificial intelligence, professional practice, and responsible computing within contemporary computing education (Kumar et al., 2023). Becker et al. (2023) describe AI code generation as both an opportunity and a challenge for introductory programming. Denny et al. (2024) argue that generative AI changes how computing educators must think about teaching, assessment, and academic integrity. The issue is therefore not whether learners will encounter AI tools, but whether they will learn to use them with enough understanding to evaluate their output.

Evidence from novice-learning research is mixed in a productive way. Kazemitabaar et al. (2023) report that an AI code generator can support code-authoring performance for novice learners while also examining what happens when learners later modify code. That result supports careful use of assistance, but it does not mean that every learner will understand generated code or transfer the practice to a new setting. UNESCO guidance calls for human-centred use, privacy protection, and institutional capacity to evaluate whether generative AI is educationally and ethically appropriate (Miao & Holmes, 2023). The OECD (2023) likewise describes digital education as an ecosystem involving tools, institutions, teaching practice, assessment, and the people who make those elements work together.

For a beginner-focused webinar, these sources favor a sequence that moves from concept to example to verification. Learners should see what an AI tool proposes, why a developer might accept or reject it, how a test exposes an error, and how human judgment remains involved. That sequence also provides a reasonable basis for post-event evaluation: participants can comment on clarity, relevance, demonstrations, and usefulness, while the study must avoid treating those perceptions as direct evidence of skill acquisition.

## Synthesis and research gap

The literature converges on a bounded instructional principle: AI can support software development, but the developer must remain responsible for understanding, testing, reviewing, and deciding whether generated output is acceptable. TDD offers a visible feedback routine for teaching that responsibility. Computing-education studies explain why the routine must be adapted for novices, and risk-management guidance explains why the session should include limits and oversight rather than tool enthusiasm alone.

The supplied literature also leaves a practical gap for this report. The sources address software quality, TDD, AI-tool interaction, automated testing, software-engineering benchmarks, and novice education, but they do not provide evidence about this particular webinar or its respondents. The present study therefore makes a modest contribution: it documents how one organized webinar was received through a convergent evaluation of normalized Likert responses and open-ended feedback. It does not claim to measure objective learning, compare instructional treatments, or generalize to all beginners. The literature review thus supplies the interpretive frame for the results while keeping the webinar’s own evidence and its limits distinct.
